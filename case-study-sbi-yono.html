SBI YONO Business — Redesigning the Profile as a Control Center

What looked like "just a profile screen" turned out to be one of the most complex design challenges I've faced — a control center that needed to work for seven completely different types of corporate banking users.

There's no such thing as "just a profile screen"

SBI YONO Business is a unified digital platform serving corporate and business banking customers across India — handling everything from payments to trade finance to cash management, for solo entrepreneurs to large organisations.

I was brought in as part of a larger effort to revamp the entire YONO Business experience. My piece of the puzzle? Profile Management.

The existing profile screen was minimal. Users could see their name, email, mobile number, and not much else. The task seemed simple enough — enhance the profile section, give users more control and information.

It was not simple. Not even close.

Seven types of users walk into a bank…

YONO Business doesn't just have users — it has seven distinct types, each with completely different needs, permissions, and mental models of what "their profile" even means.

01
Saral User

Individual business customers with simple, self-contained needs

02
Vistaar Admin

Administrators managing teams and delegating permissions across an organisation

03
Transaction Users

Operators executing payments within defined authority limits

04
Regulators

Senior users with oversight and approval authority at the top of the hierarchy

05
Authorisers

Users who approve transactions before they are processed

06
Viewers

Read-only users with visibility but no transactional authority

07
Makers

Users who initiate transactions for authoriser review

My job: design one profile system that works for all of them. Easy, right?

Constraint · No access to real data

No access to analytics data — the client wasn't comfortable sharing usage data. That meant flying blind unless I talked directly to users. So I did.

Talking to real people

I interviewed 8–10 users across different user types and had multiple conversations with stakeholders to understand both user needs and business requirements. Six major pain points surfaced consistently.

Pain point 01

Critical information was invisible

The existing profile showed basic info but users couldn't see the critical information they actually needed to do their job.

High severity
Pain point 02

Zero organisational visibility

Users in complex hierarchies had no visibility into their organisational structure — who was above them, who reported to them, who could authorise what.

High severity
Pain point 03

Scattered security controls

Password management, 2FA, and IP restrictions existed but were poorly organised — users couldn't find what they needed when they needed it.

Medium severity
Pain point 04

No permission visibility

Role-based users had no visibility into their permissions or authorisation limits — they couldn't tell what they were and weren't allowed to do.

High severity
Pain point 05

Hidden transaction limits

Users didn't know their daily or per-transaction limits, causing failed transactions and unnecessary support contacts.

High severity
Pain point 06

No approval transparency

No indication whether profile updates needed approval or would happen instantly — users were left uncertain after taking action.

Medium severity

The aha moment

We weren't just redesigning a profile screen. We were designing a control center that would help users understand their place in complex organisational hierarchies.

Once I understood the complexity, I knew a one-size-fits-all approach wouldn't cut it. I needed multiple variations of the profile screen — but they couldn't feel like completely different products.

The first thing I did was organise all my research into FigJam and start grouping pain points by theme. That's when the structure became clear: the answer wasn't more screens, it was a smarter system.

The big question

Seven separate profiles, or one flexible system?

Option A — Seven separate profiles: Design a distinct profile experience for each user type. Maximum tailoring, maximum maintenance burden, inevitable drift between versions over time.

Option B — One modular system: Design a single adaptive framework where common elements stay consistent and role-specific sections appear only when relevant to the logged-in user.

Decision · Option B, with a twist

A modular system that adapts intelligently to who's logged in

Common elements — name, contact info, security settings — stay consistent across all user types. Role-specific elements — permissions, approval workflows, team hierarchy — appear only for users who need them. A Saral user wouldn't see "Team management." A Vistaar admin would.

The interface assembles itself from one component library rather than maintaining seven parallel products.

Sketching the information architecture

Based on user needs and the existing four-tab structure, I restructured the information architecture into a clearer, more logical hierarchy — one that reflected how users actually think about their profile, not how the system was built.

From chaos to clarity — wireframing

I started with lo-fi sketches and went through multiple rounds of internal review with the team. I tested different layouts and components through A/B testing, and ran guerrilla testing to see which approaches felt more intuitive before committing to high-fidelity work.

Design decisions — three that shaped the outcome

Decision 01 · Navigation structure

Left navigation over single-page scroll

Option 1 — Left navigation: A sidebar organising information into distinct categories — Profile, Security, Payment, Settings — grouping related information together and providing clear mental models for where to find specific features.

Option 2 — Single-page layout: All information accessible through one continuous scroll, with Primary Contact & Authority, Business Information, and User Hierarchy & Management visible at once.

We went with Option 1. This structure scales better as features are added and reduces cognitive load — users focus on one category at a time instead of processing everything in a single overwhelming scroll.

Decision 02 · Team hierarchy visualisation

Flowchart over collapsible list — with an expandable twist

Option 1 — Collapsible list: A text-based tree showing the Regulator at the top, with expandable sections for Administrators and Transaction Users. Each card shows name, designation, department, employee ID, contact, and permissions.

Option 2 — Flowchart visualisation: A diagram showing hierarchical relationships with connecting lines branching from high authority down through Administrators to Transaction Users.

We went with Option 2 — but with an expandable view. At a glance, users can see who reports to whom, how many people sit under each admin, and where they fit in the organisation. The expandable detail layer adds depth without cluttering the overview.

Decision 03 · Role-based adaptability

Designing for role-based adaptability — the trickiest part

The interface needed to be smart enough to show and hide sections based on user type without feeling inconsistent or confusing. I created a matrix mapping what appears for whom and why — this became the single source of truth for what the modular system needed to support.

Visual design — what shipped

Screen 01 · Identity & organisational visibility

Visibility of required information

This section addresses the core identity and organisational visibility needs. Users can now see comprehensive personal details, access company information, and understand their team structure — all in one place.

Screen 02 · Team hierarchy

Understanding team structure at a glance

A visual org chart shows the complete hierarchy from high authority down through three administrators to transaction users. Role badges provide instant recognition, and summary metrics at the top give a quick team snapshot without requiring deep navigation.

Screen 03 · Security & payment settings

Consolidated security access

Instead of being scattered across different areas, all payment settings are now consolidated in one place — making it easy for users to enable, disable, or manage payment services as needed. Security controls (password, 2FA, IP restrictions) live here too, organised as a coherent set of controls rather than a list of disconnected options.

Screen 04 · Permissions & authorisation

Visibility into permissions & authorisation limits

Quick transfers goes deeper by showing authorisation management — users can see exactly who can authorise transfers, with their details and limits displayed clearly. This visibility helps users understand not just their own permissions, but how authorisation works across their entire team.

Screen 05 · Notifications

Account-level notification control

Users can now control SMS notifications for each account individually with simple toggles. Bulk "Disable all" and "Enable all" actions handle multiple accounts quickly, while search and pagination manage large account lists. An Important Notes sidebar clarifies OTP requirements and prevents confusion about duplicate messages.

Testing

What worked

Users could finally see the information they'd been asking for. The role-based sections made sense without overwhelming simpler user types. Navigation felt natural even with all the new features. Security management was actually understandable.

What needed tweaking

Some terminology needed adjustment based on how users actually talked about things. A few sections needed reordering based on what people looked for first. Minor layout refinements for better readability.

After incorporating the feedback and getting final approval from stakeholders, I handed everything off to the development team.

Final thought

This project taught me that the most interesting design challenges aren't always the flashiest ones. Taking something as fundamental as a profile screen and transforming it into a genuinely useful tool for thousands of corporate banking users — that's the kind of work that quietly matters.

In enterprise design, there's no such thing as "just a profile screen."

Would I underestimate the complexity next time? Hopefully not.
