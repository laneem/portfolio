Managing Users, Made Effortless

SBI Yono Business serves corporate and business banking customers across India — from solo entrepreneurs to large organizations. I redesigned the Profile Management system to serve seven distinct user types through one intelligent, modular interface.

The Problem

A profile screen that was anything but simple

The existing profile screen was minimal — users could see their name, email, and phone number, and not much else. But Yono Business doesn't just have users — it has seven distinct types, each with completely different needs. My job was to design one Profile system that works for all of them. No access to analytics data made user interviews essential.

The Aha Moment

Not a profile screen. A control centre.

Once I understood the complexity, I knew a one-size-fits-all approach wouldn't work. The key insight: we weren't redesigning a profile screen. We were designing a control centre that helps users understand their place in complex organisational hierarchies. I chose a modular system — one where common elements stayed consistent, and role-specific elements appeared only for users who needed them.

Key Decisions

Three decisions that changed the experience

Decision 1 — Navigation pattern: Left navigation over single-page scroll. It scales better as features are added and reduces cognitive load by letting users focus on one category at a time.

Decision 2 — Hierarchy visualisation: A flowchart-style org chart with expandable views, over a collapsible list. At a glance, users see who reports to whom and where they fit.

Decision 3 — Role-based adaptability: A matrix defining what appears for each user type. A Saral user never sees "Team Management" — a Vistaar admin always does.

Reflection

There's no such thing as "just a profile screen"

This project taught me that the most interesting design challenges aren't always the flashiest ones. Taking something as fundamental as a profile screen and transforming it into a genuinely useful tool for thousands of corporate banking users — that's the kind of work that quietly matters. Would I underestimate the complexity next time? Hopefully not.
