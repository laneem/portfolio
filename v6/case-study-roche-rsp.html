Summary

Roche's Rave Support Portal (RSP) supports clinical-trial data entry — a context where data has to be accurate, traceable, and entered under regulatory scrutiny. As the sole designer, I reworked the experience to keep support teams oriented through long, exacting workflows: timely updates and alerts, bookmarks to hold their place, a help layer that responds to context, and clear guidance to the next step. The work produced measurable improvements in clinical operations.

Context — why this mattered

Clinical-trial data entry sits on the critical path of getting a therapy through a trial. Errors don't just cost time — they generate queries, audit risk, and delays that ripple across a study. The support teams doing this work move through long, detailed forms where it's easy to lose your place, miss an update, or stall waiting for the right answer. Every one of those friction points is a quality risk in a regulated environment.

In clinical operations, helping someone keep their place is a data-integrity feature, not a convenience.

My role

I was a one-person design team on RSP — effectively owning the whole experience while partnering tightly with engineering and clinical operations.

  • Owned: research with support teams, the workflow redesign, the updates/alerts/help/bookmark systems, and the full UI through to handoff.
  • Owned: the prioritisation calls on what to fix first given limited design capacity and a regulated, change-controlled environment.
  • Collaborated: with engineering on feasibility within the existing portal, and with clinical-operations stakeholders on what the data and the regulations required.

Research & synthesis

Through contextual inquiry and usability testing with support staff, the pattern was consistent: people weren't failing at data entry, they were losing continuity. They'd lose their place in a long form, miss that something upstream had changed, or break flow to hunt for help — and each interruption is where errors creep in.

Synthesis reframed the problem from "make the form nicer" to "keep the operator oriented." The design needed to protect attention and continuity across long, exacting tasks — that became the brief.

Design decisions

Each decision traded raw simplicity for the things that actually protect accuracy in a regulated workflow.

Tension · A clean screen vs. never missing a change

Updates & alerts that surface, without shouting

In a trial portal, a missed update is a quality problem; but alert overload trains people to ignore alerts. Ruled out: a blanket notification feed. I designed updates and alerts to surface what changed and what needs the operator's attention, prioritised so the signal stays trustworthy — present enough to be relied on, restrained enough to keep meaning.

Tension · Linear forms vs. real, interrupted work

Bookmarks to hold a place in long workflows

Long data-entry tasks get interrupted — that's reality, not an edge case. Rather than pretend the work is uninterrupted, I added bookmarks so operators can hold and return to an exact position without losing context or re-deriving where they were. Designing for interruption is what keeps accuracy intact across a real workday.

Tension · Static documentation vs. answers in context

A dynamic help section + guidance to the next step

Sending people to a separate manual breaks flow exactly when they're least able to afford it. Ruled out: static, off-to-the-side help. I built help that responds to where the user is, paired with clear "next step" guidance, so the answer and the path forward arrive in context instead of requiring a detour.

Reflection

What worked: reframing from "improve the form" to "protect continuity" gave the project a sharper target and led to features that measurably helped operations rather than just tidying the UI.

What didn't work / what I'd change: the portal carried real legacy debt — maintenance was a nightmare and the experience had grown inconsistent over years of additions. Working solo inside that, I sometimes patched the surface where the system needed structural cleanup. Next time I'd push earlier for a small consistency pass on the underlying patterns, even at the cost of slower visible progress, because inconsistency in a regulated tool is a slow, compounding risk.
