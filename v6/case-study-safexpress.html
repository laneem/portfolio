Breaking the bottleneck: reimagining Safexpress's inbound unloading

The dock was the hidden constraint on the whole operation. I redesigned the unloading experience for people working fast, on their feet, not at a desk.

Summary

Inbound unloading at the dock was slowing everything downstream — once a vehicle backs up at intake, the whole facility waits. As the solo designer on a six-person team, I redesigned the unloading experience around how warehouse staff actually move: fast scanning, quick category access, and a live view of vehicle progress. The result was a fully functional system and a client team that came out of the project with new design and product skills.

Context — why this mattered

In logistics, the dock is a throughput valve. Inbound unloading sets the pace for put-away, sorting, and outbound — so a slow, error-prone intake doesn't stay a local problem, it taxes the entire network. The existing experience assumed a deskbound, deliberate user. The real user is standing at a vehicle, under time pressure, often with one hand busy and no patience for a flow that wasn't built for the floor.

The bottleneck wasn't the people — it was an interface that fought how they actually work.

My role

I was the solo designer embedded in a six-person product team, which meant I owned the full arc and partnered closely across functions.

  • Owned: research on the floor, the IA for inbound items, the unloading and scanning flows, the vehicle-progress model, and the full UI.
  • Owned: running the design thinking with the client and bringing their team along — part of the deliverable was capability, not just screens.
  • Collaborated: with engineering and product on feasibility and sequencing, and with client stakeholders on operational rules and edge cases.

Research & synthesis

I used contextual inquiry to watch unloading happen, not to ask people to describe it after the fact — the gap between the two is where the bottleneck lived. Usability testing then pressure-tested the flow against real speed, and affinity mapping turned the observations into a small set of requirements.

The core insight: staff don't browse, they hunt. They need to get to the right category and confirm a scan in as few moves as possible, and they need to know where each vehicle stands without asking anyone. Anything that adds a tap or a moment of doubt compounds across hundreds of items per shift.

Design decisions

Designing for the floor is a constant trade between completeness and speed. Three decisions defined the system.

Tension · Findability vs. fewest taps

Category search & tabs over deep menus

A complete inbound catalogue wants structure, but deep navigation is death on the floor. Ruled out: nested category trees. I combined search with a flat set of tabs so staff can either jump straight to a known category or filter fast — findability without the drilling.

Tension · Data accuracy vs. scanning speed

Quick scanning with recovery built in

The fastest scan flow is useless if a mis-scan stops the line. I designed scanning for speed first and made error-recovery immediate and in-context, so a wrong scan is a half-second correction, not a trip back through a flow. Contextual quick actions sit right where the work happens, so the next step is always one move away.

Tension · Detail vs. situational awareness

Vehicle progress as the anchor view

Supervisors and staff kept asking the same question — "where is this vehicle?" I made vehicle progress a first-class, glanceable view so the state of the dock is legible without interrupting anyone. The detail is available; the default is awareness.

Reflection

What worked: anchoring the design in floor observation, not stakeholder description, is what kept the flows honest about speed and error.

What didn't work / what I'd change: we ran the whiteboarding sessions with the client team but not with the exact end users doing the unloading. That kept us one step removed from the real constraint, and a couple of assumptions only surfaced in testing that earlier user contact would have caught sooner. Next time I'd put the actual floor users in the room from the first session — proximity to the real user is worth more than a tidier stakeholder workshop.
