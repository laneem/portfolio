Summary

SBI's YONO Business platform needed a cleaner way to manage role-based access and user administration — who can do what, granted and revoked safely, across an enterprise's worth of users. As the solo designer on a six-person team, I designed a modular interface that adapts intelligently to whoever is logged in: shared elements stay constant, role-specific elements appear by permission. The lesson of the project: the highest-value design challenges aren't always the flashiest ones.

Context — why this mattered

In enterprise banking, access control is governance. A permission granted to the wrong person isn't an inconvenience — it's an audit finding and a security exposure. But the people doing the granting are administrators under load, and an access console that's confusing produces exactly the errors it's meant to prevent. The business need was a system that makes the safe action also the easy one.

When the cost of a wrong click is compliance, clarity isn't polish — it's the control.

My role

Solo designer on a six-person product team, owning the experience end to end.

  • Owned: the IA of roles and permissions, the modular component model, the adaptive logic for what each role sees, and the full admin UI.
  • Owned: the usability validation with administrators and the rationale brought to stakeholders on where to constrain versus where to flex.
  • Collaborated: with engineering on how the permission model mapped to the interface, and with product/compliance stakeholders on the rules that couldn't bend.

Research & synthesis

I ran contextual inquiry with administrators and usability testing on the existing flows. The recurring failure wasn't that people couldn't find features — it was that everyone saw everything, including actions they weren't permitted to take, which created noise, hesitation, and the occasional dangerous attempt.

Synthesis pointed to a structural fix rather than a cosmetic one: stop building one console for all roles and start building one system that renders the right console per role. The interface should reflect the permission model, not paper over it.

Design decisions

The discipline here was restraint — the right move was a clean model, consistently applied, not a clever interface.

Tension · One interface for all vs. tailored per role

A modular system: common elements + role-specific elements

Designing a separate screen per role doesn't scale and drifts out of sync; designing one screen for everyone overwhelms and misleads. Ruled out: both extremes — bespoke-per-role and one-size-fits-all. I split the interface into common elements every administrator shares and role-specific elements that appear by permission, so the console is assembled from one library rather than maintained as many.

Tension · Show-and-disable vs. adapt-to-context

The interface adapts to who's logged in

Showing actions a user can't take — greyed out — is honest but adds clutter and tempts mistakes in a context where mistakes are costly. I had the interface adapt to the signed-in role so people primarily see what's theirs to do, with access boundaries communicated clearly rather than left as dead controls. The console matches the user's authority.

Tension · Speed vs. safety on destructive actions

Make granting fast, make revoking and risk legible

Routine administration should be quick, but access changes carry weight. I kept the common path efficient while making the consequence of a change visible at the moment of action, so administrators move fast on the safe stuff and slow down exactly where the system needs them to.

Reflection

What worked: treating this as a systems problem — one adaptive model instead of many screens — is what made the result both safer and more maintainable.

What didn't work / what I'd change: the legacy starting point was a maintenance nightmare with an inconsistent experience built up over time, and untangling that mid-build cost more than expected. If I started again I'd invest earlier in nailing the permission-to-UI mapping with engineering before designing screens, so the modular model is locked before anything visual depends on it — that's the decision that keeps a system like this from drifting back into inconsistency.
