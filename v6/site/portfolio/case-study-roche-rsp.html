One platform for all Rave clinical trial support

The Rave Support Portal (RSP) is the global platform used by Roche's clinical operations teams to manage data-entry workflows across Rave — the industry-standard EDC system for clinical trial data. Clinical teams rely on RSP to handle form submission, track trial updates, and coordinate across studies running simultaneously worldwide.

The problem: despite being central to trial operations, RSP had grown fragmented. Forms were siloed. Updates were buried. Help was nowhere near where the work happened. Teams were spending time navigating the system rather than running trials.

The workflows were fragmented, silent, and blind

Research with clinical operations users surfaced a consistent pattern: the system had never been designed around how people actually work. Six recurring pain points emerged across interviews and contextual inquiry sessions.

Problem 01

No form guidance

Users had no indication of where to start or what the priority order of forms was. Every session began with orientation overhead before any actual work could begin.

Problem 02

Siloed form duplication

The same forms were being created and filled multiple times by different team members with no visibility across users. Duplication was invisible until the audit stage — by which point it created rework.

Problem 03

No access to frequent tools

Power users who worked with the same set of forms daily had no way to save or quickly access their most-used workflows. Every session meant navigating from scratch.

Problem 04

Manual user form documentation

Users maintained offline notes and spreadsheets to track form requirements and field definitions — work the system should have been doing. This documentation was inconsistent, outdated, and non-transferable.

Problem 05

Trial updates buried in workflow

Changes to trial protocols, form requirements, or data queries were only visible mid-form — meaning users discovered important updates after already committing time to an outdated version of the task.

Problem 06

High cognitive load for users

With no in-context help, no smart defaults, and no guidance between tasks, users carried all the cognitive load themselves. Even experienced operators described the system as exhausting to use.

Four decisions that changed the experience

Each decision addressed one of the core fragmentation problems — surfacing what was hidden, shortcutting what was slow, and reducing what was unnecessary.

Decision 01 · Updates & Alerts

Updates and alerts — visible the moment you log in

Previously, trial updates and data queries only surfaced inside the form workflow — meaning users discovered them mid-task, after already committing time to the wrong version of the work. Ruled out: an email-based notification system (creates a separate context-switch and gets buried). The new RSP surfaces personalised alerts and trial updates on the login dashboard, prioritised so the most time-sensitive items appear first. Users know what needs attention before they open a single form.

  • Personalised alert feed visible immediately on login
  • Updates scoped to the user's assigned trials and forms
  • Priority ordering — data queries and protocol changes surface above routine updates
  • Dismissed alerts archived, not deleted — accessible when needed
[ IMAGE — RSP login dashboard with personalised alerts and trial update feed ]
Decision 02 · Bookmarks

Bookmark your most-used forms for instant access

Power users were navigating the same set of forms every day from a flat, unsorted list. Ruled out: auto-surfacing based purely on recency (recent ≠ frequent for clinical workflows). I introduced a bookmark system — users pin the forms they return to most, and RSP surfaces them as a dedicated "Frequently used" section on the dashboard. The order is editable. New users see a guided prompt to set up their list; experienced users skip straight to work.

  • Bookmark any form with a single click from the form list
  • "Frequently used" section appears at the top of the dashboard
  • Drag-to-reorder for personal priority sequencing
  • Bookmarks persist and sync across sessions
[ IMAGE — RSP dashboard with bookmarked forms section and drag-to-reorder ]
Decision 03 · Contextual Help

Dynamic, field-aware help — right where the user needs it

Users maintained their own offline notes to understand what fields meant and what data was acceptable. The existing static documentation was not linked to any specific form or field context. Ruled out: a general help panel docked to the side (doesn't solve the field-level ambiguity problem). I built field-aware help triggered by a contextual icon on each field — the help content adapts to the specific field, explains the required data format, common mistakes to avoid, and includes the relevant regulatory reference where applicable.

  • Help icon on every field — no separate page or manual required
  • Content is field-specific, not generic
  • Covers: what to enter, accepted formats, common errors, and regulatory context
  • Expandable inline — doesn't break the form flow
[ IMAGE — RSP form field with expanded contextual help layer ]
Decision 04 · Guided Progression

After one form, you're guided to the next

Form completion had no momentum. After submitting, users returned to a flat list with no indication of what to do next. In a clinical trial workflow, forms are sequenced — completing one typically creates the context for the next. Ruled out: a rigid wizard that forces linear progression (trials are non-linear; guidance needs to be suggestive, not mandatory). I added a "What's next" prompt on the completion screen — surfacing the logically connected form or action based on the workflow pattern. Users can follow the suggestion or navigate freely.

  • Guided "next step" appears on every form completion screen
  • Suggestion is workflow-aware — based on trial stage and what's already done
  • Always optional — users can dismiss and navigate anywhere else
  • Eliminates the "now what?" drop-off moment after submission

Principles that shaped every decision

Working in a regulated clinical environment surfaces constraints that don't exist in consumer design — accuracy is non-negotiable, traceability is required, and the cost of a wrong design decision is measured in delayed trials, not dropped sessions.

01
Believe before you see

Surface what the user needs before they know they need it. Proactive information — alerts, updates, suggested next steps — reduces the cognitive cost of staying oriented across complex, ongoing trials.

02
Smarter user launches

The moment of login sets the tone for the session. A dashboard that immediately shows what's urgent, what's bookmarked, and what's changed turns a cold start into a warm handoff from the previous session.

03
Prioritise on predictability

Clinical users build muscle memory. Consistent patterns, stable layouts, and reliable interactions reduce the mental overhead of using the system — freeing attention for the trial work itself.

04
Commitment by design

In a regulated environment, every design decision needs to be defensible — not just to users, but to compliance and clinical stakeholders. Documenting rationale behind decisions turned out to be as important as the decisions themselves.

What looked like a navigation problem was actually a continuity problem. Once users could stay oriented across sessions, the quality metrics followed.

What enterprise healthcare UX taught me

Learning 01

Complexity shapes design vocabulary

Clinical trial systems have a domain language that users are fluent in and designers are not. Early on I tried to simplify terminology in ways that made sense to a UX audience but confused clinical operations staff. The lesson: in expert-domain products, your job is to reduce interface complexity, not domain complexity. Use their words.

Learning 02

Power users define the roadmap

The users doing the highest volume of work exposed the deepest problems. Casual users had adapted around the system's limitations; power users hadn't — their workarounds (offline spreadsheets, copy-pasted notes, browser bookmarks) were a direct map of every gap in the product. In enterprise systems, start research with power users, not average users.

Learning 03

Prioritise decision fatigue

Clinical ops staff make hundreds of small, consequential decisions every session. Every unnecessary choice the system forces on them — what to open next, where the help lives, what this field means — is cognitive tax on people who can't afford it. The most impactful thing design can do in this context is remove decisions, not redesign them.

Learning 04

Personalisation as predictability

In consumer products, personalisation means surprises. In clinical systems, users want the opposite: they want the system to remember their preferences and be stable. Bookmarks and saved configurations aren't personalisation features in the consumer sense — they're a form of predictability that reduces the cost of starting each session.

Learning 05

Front-load clarity

The most expensive moment to discover an ambiguity is mid-task. Help layers, format guidance, and field definitions that live at the point of entry eliminate a whole class of errors that would otherwise require audit and rework. In a regulated product, clarity at input is a quality feature, not a nice-to-have.

Learning 06

Commitment by design

Working inside a regulated environment means every design decision needs to be defensible — not just to users, but to compliance and clinical stakeholders. Documenting the rationale behind decisions (especially the ones that feel counterintuitive to non-clinical reviewers) was as important as the decisions themselves. In healthcare UX, commitment by design isn't optional.
