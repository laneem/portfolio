Unloading was where the whole operation got stuck

Safexpress operates a network of regional hubs. The inbound transshipment segment handles truck arrival, unloading, sorting, and onward dispatch. The process was clogged at the unloading stage — manual workflows, weak visibility for team leads, high error rates in scanning — delaying everything downstream.

Over the course of several weeks, I led the design effort to discover and document warehouse workflows, create intuitive mobile interfaces, conduct stakeholder workshops, and guide the team through an agile design process.

We successfully delivered a fully functional unloading management system that is currently being piloted in several warehouses, and set the foundation for additional features for the system to be scaled up.

Modernizing operations with minimal impact to familiar workflows

Unloading operations were challenged on every front — not just one problem to solve, but a cluster of interconnected failures that compounded each other across every shift.

Challenge 01

No real-time visibility

Inconsistent unloading times, limited real-time visibility, and reliance on manual coordination meant supervisors couldn't accurately track progress or intervene during delays.

High severity
Challenge 02

Scanning mismatches & system lag

Scanning errors and delayed system updates prevented supervisors from knowing where things actually stood. Reactive decision-making and frequent team idle periods were the result.

High severity
Challenge 03

Manual floor communication

Communication depended on verbal updates and phone calls, leading to confusion, misalignment across teams, and avoidable downtime. No shared source of truth existed on the floor.

High severity
Challenge 04

Unreliable handheld application

Reliability and usability issues with the handheld app disrupted workflows and reduced confidence in system data, further impacting productivity and data accuracy.

High severity
Challenge 05

No performance metrics

The lack of clear performance metrics and real-time dashboards made effective monitoring and accountability across shifts impossible. Insufficient automation and digital transparency created operational inefficiencies across the board.

Medium severity

Ride-along research leads to empathy, and uncovering opportunities

To observe and document workflows across different functional areas, the team and I split up to shadow employees in different parts of the warehouse. Over the course of a few days, we gathered dozens of photos, pages of notes, and hours of video.

Most revealing were the various paper-based workarounds that had been created to fill gaps in the existing system. These weren't failures of the people — they were signals of where the system had let them down.

User interviews — four roles, one broken system

We conducted structured interviews across the key roles involved in hub unloading to surface the pain points behind the paper trails.

01
Team Member — Unloader (Primary User)

Assignments were unclear until verbally communicated. Multiple clicks were needed for each scan, slowing the workflow. Frequent network drops forced rescans. Users wanted a simple, fast interface with minimal steps.

02
Experienced Team Member (4+ yrs)

Reverted to paper notes when the app failed to sync. Worked faster with visual cues and fewer forms. Wanted sound/vibration feedback for successful scans to confirm without looking at the screen.

03
New Team Member (joined < 6 months)

Found the app too technical and confusing. Icons were unclear and actions took too long. Needed a step-by-step guided flow — no assumed knowledge, no hidden steps.

04
Shift Lead — Field Supervisor

Relied on manual headcounts and verbal updates. Had no live visibility of who was idle or busy. Wanted system-generated task rotation and alerts to replace the radio-and-shouting coordination model.

Three opportunity areas the interviews revealed

01
Real-time transparency — a live dashboard for supervisors and a visible queue for team members
02
Simplified workflow — reduced navigation steps and offline scan caching for network dead zones
03
Empowered users — self-assigned next tasks and performance tracking for motivation

Persona — Transshipment Inbound Team Member

Journey map — TL & TM (Unloading)

During the stakeholder workshop and hub visit, the team provided a walkthrough focusing on how goods are received and unloaded at the hub. The session helped visualise the complete operational flow — from arrival and dock allocation through to unloading, sorting, and movement to outbound areas.

While the functional team on the client side began building user flows, the design team created a scalable navigation layout and design system with basic components for desktop and mobile formats — specifically for Zebra handheld scanner devices running Android.

Soon, the team was creating wireframes. With the design system in place, we were able to quickly move into UI mock-ups.

Pivot point — hitting a wall with user stories

The sprint cycle kept running into roadblocks

We began running into issues with the user stories. The sprint cycle kept hitting roadblocks validating requirements, which left the design team blocked. As it turned out, observing employees and learning their workflows did not translate into robust, design-ready user stories. The client's functional team was new to Agile methodology and needed more support to be successful. We decided to take a pause and re-assess the project scope and needs.

Product visioning workshops pave the way for a clearer future

Over the course of a week, the project leadership and I led a series of visioning workshops to level-set on the scope and deep-dive into each functional area. Armed with several big whiteboards, I facilitated discussions with the client's functional team and subject-matter experts to define Happy Paths and Unhappy Paths for each area. We then did high-level sketches of key screens.

Having all this information made visible helped align all stakeholders and gave the design team more solid requirements to work from.

Since the week-long visioning workshops were not initially part of the project timeline, I also reworked the design team timeline to account for the change in scope as well as an extension of the project.

With these workshops complete, I was able to more confidently educate the client's functional team on Agile best practices and help them write clear user stories. The design and dev teams could then efficiently iterate on designs and build features with fewer blockers.

A versatile and robust system that follows UX best practices

The unloading dashboard gives team members everything they need from the moment they start a session — designed so that finding trucks, scanning waybills, and checking status is straightforward, with a clear information hierarchy optimised for handheld devices under real working conditions.

Feature 01 · Category Search & Tabs

A clear truck list with priority indicators

Team members see a clear list of trucks assigned to them for the shift with priority indicators — so they always know what to work on next without asking a supervisor. Search and tabs let them jump to a known category or filter fast, without navigating deep menus.

[ IMAGE — category search and tabs view on handheld device ]
Feature 02 · Quick Scanning

Streamlined waybill scanning with built-in exception handling

The scanning flow is built for speed first. For damaged or mislabelled packages, exception handling is immediate and in-context — a wrong scan is a half-second correction, not a trip back through a flow. Contextual quick actions sit right where the work happens, so the next step is always one move away.

[ IMAGE — quick scanning screen on handheld: waybill entry, package count, exception state ]
Feature 03 · Vehicle Progress

Final sign-off and glanceable dock status

Supervisors and team members can see where every vehicle stands at a glance — waybills scanned, packages counted, status. Final sign-off and status change happens here once all items are unloaded and verified. The state of the dock is legible without interrupting anyone's work.

[ IMAGE — vehicle progress view: truck status, waybill/package tally, sign-off action ]

The unloading flow end to end

The full flow takes a team member from starting an unloading session through to completion and handoff — four states, each designed to keep the work moving without requiring decisions that shouldn't be theirs to make.

Step 01 Scan Waybills Open session · select truck · begin scanning waybill by waybill
Step 02 Summary Live tally — waybills scanned, packages counted, exceptions flagged
Step 03 Pending Waybill List Review outstanding items, resolve exceptions, confirm completeness
Step 04 Complete Unloading confirmed · sent to team lead for approval · ready for next task

Addressing the elephant in the room can take up time, but saves more time in the future

When the team first decided to pause to conduct the product visioning workshops, I was worried about how much that would impact our timelines. The design team was already behind schedule on several features due to churn with the requirements.

However, stopping to re-visit the scope and user journeys ended up being exactly what we needed to move faster. The one-week pause in designing led to much more clarity and greatly reduced the number of iterations needed for each design flow. The project leadership recognised the need and were on board with extending the overall timeline, affording us the space and resources to do our best work.

Sometimes, the fastest way to move forward is to stop and take a look around first.
