Case 01 · Telecom

Enabling multi-brand consistency through a unified selfcare platform

One self-service experience, several Telefónica brands — each keeping its identity, none rebuilt from scratch.

Summary

Telefónica needed a single selfcare platform — where customers manage plans, billing, and support — to serve multiple brands at once. The constraint: each brand had to feel like itself, but the team couldn't afford to design and maintain a separate product for every one. I led UX on a task-first, themeable system that one product team can ship and extend across brands instead of forking the experience four ways.

Context — why this mattered

Selfcare is where a telecom either deflects a support call or causes one. Every task a customer can't finish online becomes a call centre cost and a churn risk. Telefónica's challenge was that it operates several brands, and the obvious path — a bespoke product per brand — multiplies build cost, fragments quality, and guarantees the brands drift apart over time.

The business question underneath the design brief was really: how do we get the economics of one platform with the market presence of many brands? That framing is what made this a systems problem before it was a screens problem.

If consistency is the goal, the unit of design can't be the screen — it has to be the pattern.

My role

I led UX, embedded in a four-person product team alongside a PM and engineering. What I specifically owned:

  • Owned: the end-to-end selfcare flows, the task-first IA, the themeable component and state system, and the usability-testing programme that validated it.
  • Owned: the design decisions and the rationale taken to stakeholders — including which behaviours stayed shared across brands and which were allowed to vary.
  • Collaborated: with the PM on prioritisation and scope, and with engineering on how theming was implemented so the design system mapped cleanly to tokens in code.

Research & synthesis

I ran contextual inquiry and usability testing with real customers across brands, and audited the existing flows for where people stalled. Two findings shaped everything that followed.

One: across brands, customers wanted the same handful of things first — check a bill, change a plan, fix a problem. The brand mattered for trust and tone, not for the structure of the task. Two: people didn't read the home screen as a brochure; they scanned it for the one job they came to do, and bounced to the call centre the moment that job wasn't obvious.

Affinity mapping the sessions made the split clear: a layer of shared behaviour (what the tasks are and how they flow) sitting under a layer of brand expression (how it looks and sounds). That separation became the spine of the system.

Design decisions

The hard part wasn't drawing screens — it was deciding where to hold the line on consistency and where to let brands breathe. Three decisions carried the project.

Tension · Brand freedom vs. one maintainable system

Task-first home, brand-themed — not brand-first home, task-buried

The pull from the business was a richly branded landing experience per brand. I ruled that out as the organising principle: it buries the tasks customers actually come for and forces a different layout per brand. Considered and dropped: per-brand bespoke homepages. Instead I made the home task-first — the top jobs surfaced immediately — and let brand live in theming, tone, and imagery layered over a shared structure. Brands stay distinct where it counts (identity) and identical where it pays off (behaviour and maintenance).

Tension · Onboarding completeness vs. getting out of the way

Guided onboarding that can be skipped, not a wall

Selfcare onboarding wants to teach and verify, but every extra step is a place to lose someone who just wants to pay a bill. I designed a guided flow that scaffolds first-time users through setup and contextual quick actions that let returning users jump straight to a task. Ruled out: a mandatory linear onboarding gate. The guidance is present, not imposed.

Tension · Pretty mockups vs. the states that actually ship

Complete state coverage as a first-class deliverable

Enterprise selfcare breaks in the unhappy states — pending, error, partial data, account on hold — and those are exactly what a per-brand fork would get wrong inconsistently. I treated empty, loading, error, and edge states as part of the component spec, not an afterthought, so every brand inherits correct behaviour by default. This is the least glamorous decision and the one that most protects consistency at scale.

Reflection

What I'd carry forward: defining the shared-vs-brand split early gave every later decision a clear test — "is this behaviour or expression?" — and that's what kept scope from sprawling.

What didn't work / what I'd change: I waited too long on a couple of decisions hoping for cleaner data that never fully arrived. The lesson I took: in a multi-brand build, don't wait for perfect data to commit to the structural decisions — make the call on the strongest evidence you have, ship it behind the system, and let real usage refine the edges. Perfect data is usually just a slower way to learn the same thing.
